When First Try rewrites your draft it applies the techniques on this page and no others. Each one lists where it comes from. The score is a formula, not an opinion: it starts from the strongest source (our own tests 0.90, a research paper 0.70, a vendor's guide 0.60, a house rule 0.45, a community source 0.30), rises by 0.10 for each further independent publisher, falls by 0.20 for each source that contradicts it, is multiplied by 0.8 for advice about one model family that has had no fresh support for a year, moves by up to 0.20 when a measurement shows a clear effect, and is not used below 0.40 on its sources alone.

We keep findings in our own words with a short quotation and a link. We do not copy source documents.

Rules built into every rewrite

Phrase constraints as what to do, not what to avoid.

Evidence: Strong (0.80) · any task · any model

  • arXiv, Principled Instructions Are All You Need (Bsharat, Myrzakhan & Shen) (research paper). Principle 4: use affirmative directives.
  • Anthropic, Prompting best practices (vendor guide). Advises telling the model what to do instead of what not to do.

Give four to eight checkable quality criteria drawn from the checklist for this kind of task.

Evidence: Good (0.70) · any task · any model

  • arXiv, Principled Instructions Are All You Need (Bsharat, Myrzakhan & Shen) (research paper). Principle 25: clearly state the requirements the model must follow.
  • arXiv, Less Back-and-Forth: A Comparative Study of Structured Prompting (research paper). Checklist-improved prompts scored highest (7.50 of 8) and used the fewest tokens.

Ask a clarifying question only when the draft is ambiguous; never on a clear one.

Evidence: Strong (0.95) · any task · any model

  • First Try, First Try eval, 37 conversations, 9 November 2026 (our own test). The gate asked on 60% of ambiguous drafts and on 0% of clear ones (37 cases).
  • arXiv, CLAM: Selective Clarification for Ambiguous Questions (Kuhn et al.) (research paper). Clarification helps on ambiguous inputs and penalises unambiguous ones.
  • arXiv, Less Back-and-Forth: A Comparative Study of Structured Prompting (research paper). Clarifying-question prompts scored 6.67/8 vs 7.50 for checklist prompts, and used more tokens. Checklist prompts also produced the best quality-effort tradeoff, using fewer average tokens than both raw and clarifying prompts.

Say who the result is for and carry over the relevant facts from the conversation.

Evidence: Strong (0.80) · any task · any model

  • arXiv, Principled Instructions Are All You Need (Bsharat, Myrzakhan & Shen) (research paper). Principle 2: integrate the intended audience.
  • arXiv, Conversational User-AI Intervention: A Study on Prompt Rewriting (Sarkar et al.) (research paper). Rewriting with conversation history improves responses, more so in longer conversations.
  • arXiv, Less Back-and-Forth: A Comparative Study of Structured Prompting (research paper). Checklist prompts covering context and audience scored 7.50 against 5.67 raw (0–8 rubric). Small study, single rater.
  • OpenAI, Prompt engineering guide (vendor guide) It is often useful to include additional context information the model can use to generate a response within the prompt you give the model.

End with two to four checkable criteria for when the work is done.

Split a long prompt into labelled sections or tags.

Evidence: Strong (0.90) · any task · any model

  • fabric (community), improve_prompt pattern (community) Delimiters like triple quotation marks, XML tags, section titles, etc. can help demarcate sections of text to be treated differently.
  • arXiv, Principled Instructions Are All You Need (Bsharat, Myrzakhan & Shen) (research paper). Principles 8 and 17: sections and delimiters.
  • Google, Prompt design strategies (Gemini API) (vendor guide) Employ clear delimiters to separate different parts of your prompt.
  • OpenAI, Prompt engineering guide (vendor guide). Recommends Markdown or XML delimiters and an order of identity, instructions, examples, context.
  • OpenAI, Prompt generation (vendor guide) [Optional sections with headings or bullet points for detailed steps.]
  • Anthropic, Prompting best practices (vendor guide). XML tags help the model parse complex prompts unambiguously.

Never turn a gap into an invented fact: mark each default (assumed), list the assumptions once, and leave unknowns as [placeholders].

Evidence: Strong (0.90) · any task · any model

  • First Try, First Try house rule (no outside source yet) (house rule)
  • arXiv, Conversational User-AI Intervention: A Study on Prompt Rewriting (Sarkar et al.) (research paper) LLMs often need to -- and inherently do -- make \emph{plausible} assumptions about a user's intentions and goals when interpreting prompts
  • OpenAI, Prompt generation (vendor guide). The meta-prompt preserves user content and does not add facts.

Do not add "think step by step"; ask instead for the reasoning behind the main recommendation.

Evidence: Strong (0.90) · any task · any model

  • arXiv, Better Zero-Shot Reasoning with Role-Play Prompting (Kong et al.) (research paper) role-play prompting acts as a more effective trigger for the CoT process
  • Google, Prompt design strategies (Gemini API) (vendor guide) it's generally not necessary to have the model outline, plan, or detail reasoning steps in the returned response itself.
  • OpenAI, Prompt engineering guide (vendor guide). Reasoning models do better with high-level goals than with prescribed thinking steps.
  • Anthropic, Prompting best practices (vendor guide) A prompt such as “think thoroughly” often produces better reasoning than a hand-written step-by-step plan.

Include examples only when the user supplied them, clearly delimited; otherwise ask for one with a placeholder.

Evidence: Strong (0.80) · any task · any model

  • arXiv, The Prompt Report (Schulhoff et al.) (research paper). Few-shot prompting was among the most consistently effective of 58 techniques surveyed.
  • Anthropic, Prompting best practices (vendor guide). Calls examples one of the most reliable ways to steer format, tone and structure.

Open with one or two sentences naming the specific practitioner for this task: specialty, what they are known for, what they judge by.

Evidence: Limited (0.40) · any task · any model · Not for: Factual lookups and follow-ups: personas do not improve factual accuracy.

  • arXiv, ExpertPrompting (Xu et al.) (research paper). Answers from a task-specific expert identity were preferred 48.5% against 23% for vanilla prompting (GPT-4 judge, 500 samples); a generic "imagine you are an expert" line gave no gain.
  • arXiv, Better Zero-Shot Reasoning with Role-Play Prompting (Kong et al.) (research paper). Role-play lifted ChatGPT on 12 reasoning benchmarks, e.g. AQuA 53.5% to 63.8%.
  • Anthropic, Prompting best practices (vendor guide). Recommends giving the model a role.
  • Against: arXiv, Is "A Helpful Assistant" the Best Role for Large Language Models? (Zheng et al.) (research paper). 162 roles across 2,410 factual questions: no or slightly negative effect on accuracy.
  • Against: arXiv, Prompting Science Report 4: Playing Pretend (Wharton Generative AI Labs) (research paper). In-domain expert personas had no significant impact on GPQA Diamond and MMLU-Pro accuracy across six models (except Gemini 2.0 Flash). Expert personas showed no consistent benefit across models, with few exceptions.

Every line must be checkable or change the answer; do not repeat context.

Evidence: Strong (0.80) · any task · any model

  • arXiv, The Prompt Report (Schulhoff et al.) (research paper). Duplicated context hurt accuracy in a case study.
  • arXiv, Less Back-and-Forth: A Comparative Study of Structured Prompting (research paper). The highest-scoring prompts were also the shortest.
  • OpenAI, Prompt generation (vendor guide) Clarity and Conciseness: Use clear, specific language. Avoid unnecessary instructions or bland statements.
